# 2.6 Future Scientific Databases

The examples in this chapter introduced several ways to combine language models with scientific information.

PubMed can support literature retrieval and synthesis, UniProt and the Protein Data Bank can connect protein annotations with molecular structure, and ChEMBL can provide experimental measurements of compound activity. 

Other scientific databases create additional opportunities. A language model might help translate a biological question into a structured database search, connect identifiers across resources, interpret complex metadata, compare pathway annotations, or summarize relationships within a molecular network. The most useful role of the model depends on both the scientific question and the structure of the database.

Future tutorials will explore several additional resources:
- [GEO Datasets](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gds) for analyzing public transcriptomic datasets
- [Gene Ontology (GO)](https://geneontology.org/), [Reactome](https://reactome.org/), and [KEGG](https://www.genome.jp/kegg/) for interpreting genes in terms of biological functions and pathways
- [STRING](https://string-db.org/) for exploring relationships among proteins in molecular interaction networks

These examples will focus not only on how to access each database with Python, but also on where language models can reduce the effort required to move from a scientific question to a useful analysis.

## 2.6.1 GEO: Finding Public Transcriptomic Datasets from a Research Question

The [Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) is a public repository of functional genomic studies, including bulk and single-cell RNA sequencing, microarrays, and other high-throughput experiments. GEO contains study-level record, sample metadata, processed data, and links to raw sequencing data.

For scientists, one of the most difficult parts of using GEO is often finding the right datasets. A researcher may begin with a question such as:

> *What gene expression pathways are altered in dopaminergic neurons in Parkinson's disease?*

In this tutorial, we will start with this biological question rather than a known GEO accension. We will use a language model to identify the key concepts in the question (e.g., the disease, tissue, organism, and experimental modality), and convert them into a structured GEO search. Python will then query GEO and retrieve candidate studies together with their titles, summaries, and sample metadata.

The tutorial will then compare the returned studies and identify which datasets are appropriate for the intended analysis based on factors such as sample type, disease status, assay platform, and experimental design.

The workflow will be:

```{mermaid}
flowchart TD
  A["Natural-language research question"] --> B["LLM translates question into search conepts"]
  B --> C["Structured GEO query"]
  C --> D["GEO API"]
  D --> E["Candidate GSE studies"]
  E --> F["Inspect metadata"]
  F --> G["Selected dataset"]
  G -.-> H["Downstream analysis"]
```

This example shows how language models can help scientists move from a natural-language research question to a structured database search, reducing the manual effort required to discover suitable public datasets. The selected dataset can then be passed to a downstream transcriptomic analysis workflow.

## 2.6.2 GEO, Reactome, and KEGG: Interpreting a Gene Coexpression Module

A gene coexpression analysis groups genes that show similar expression patterns into modules. These modules often receive arbitrary labels, such as "blue", that do not describe their biological roles. This raises a new question:

> *What biological process best describes the genes in this module?*

In this tutorial, we will start from a gene list from a WGCNA module. We will use Python to retrieve functional information from [Gene Ontology (GO)](https://geneontology.org/), [Reactome](https://reactome.org/), and [KEGG](https://www.kegg.jp/). We will compare the resulting terms and pathways, including which module genes support each finding.

We will then ask a language model to suggest a concise, descriptive module name and explain its choice using the retrieved evidence. This gives the scientists a pratical way to move from an arbitrary module label to a biological interpretation they can inspect and refine.

The workflow will be:

```{mermaid}
flowchart TD
  A["WGCNA module genes"] --> B["Retrieve functional evidence"]
  B --> C["GO terms"]
  B --> D["Reactome pathways"]
  B --> E["KEGG pathways"]
  C --> F["Compare terms, pathways, and supporting genes"]
  D --> F
  E --> F
  F --> G["LLM suggests a module name with evidence"]
  G -.-> H["Scientist reviews and refines the name"]
```
A module may contain hundreds of genes and return many overlapping terms and pathways. The language model helps distill that evidence into a concise interpretable name, while the supporting genes and enrichment results let scientists judge whether the name fits.

## 2.6.3 STRING: Exploring Protein Interaction Networks

Mapping the relationship between proteins within biological systems can help scientists better understand the processes involved in disease. [STRING](https://string-db.org/) brings together evidence for associations among proteins and represents those associations as a network, enabling scientists can explore questions such as:

> *Which protein networks are associated with Parkinson's disease, what evidence supports their connections, and how could we investigate them exprimentally?*

In this tutorial, we will use Python to retrieve proteins and scored associations from STRING, then build a network that highlights prominent connections and groups, and analyze the resulting network. We will give a language model the network results and supporting annotations to help identify patterns in the network, such as tightly connected nodes or proteins linking distinct groups. For promising findings, it will help trace the relevant nodes and edges back to their supporting evidence and formulate questions that can be test in the lab.

We will then use the evidence and biological context to draft follow-up expriments, including what to measure and what result would support or challenge each interpretation. The scientist will assess the proposed experiments against the strength and type of evidence behind the network.

The workflow will be:
```{mermaid}
flowchart TD
  A["Disease-related proteins"] --> B["STRING associations"]
  B --> C["Network visualization and analysis"]
  C --> D["LLM-assisted pattern detection"]
  D --> E["LLM-supported evidence review"]
  E --> F["Testable hypotheses and experiment plans"]
```

The LLM helps scientists make sense of a dense network by describing patterns, locating the evidence behind them, and turning promising observations into testable hypotheses and experiment plans.
