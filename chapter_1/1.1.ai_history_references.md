# 1.1 How Scientific AI Agents Became Possible

How did AI move from responding to questions about science to searching appers, using research tools, analyzing data, and helping guide experiment? This section follows the advances that made those leaps possible. We start with language models that can work across tasks, then trace how retrieval, tool use, and scientific evaluations led to the agents developed at FutureHouse and elsewhere.

This is a selective history focused on AI for biological research. Each subsection explains one step in the progression and includes papers and other resources for readers who want to explore it further. ## 1.1.1 From Transformers to LLMs for Science

Scientific research depends heavily on language: papers describe findings, protocols specify procedures, and database records document what is known about genes, proteins, and diseases. A useful AI system for science therefore needs to work with many kinds of technical text. 

The [transformer](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762), introduced in 2017, replaced sequential processing with self-attention, allowing models to be trained efficiently on massive text datasets.

General pretrained models like [BERT](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805) demonstrated strong language understanding. Researchers soon adapted transformer models to technical domains, including scientific and biomedical writing. For example, [BioBERT](https://academic.oup.com/bioinformatics/article/36/4/1234/5566506) improved several biomedical text mining tasks like named entity recognition and relation extraction.

Scaling transformers to tens or hundreds of billions of parameters led to **Large Language Models (LLMs)** like [GPT-3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.14165). GPT-3 demonstrated that an LLM could perform a variety of tasks using examples included in its prompt, eliminating the need to retrain a model for every new task.

[Fine-tuning LLMs with human feedback](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155) made models better at following natural language instructions, enabling scientists to interface with models using everyday conversational queries.

Together, these advances made it practical to start with a research question and use a language model across different scientific workflows. But LLMs by themselves lack access to current literature and the ability to run analyses. The sections to follow cover how researchers resolved these limitations to build functional scientific agents.

The resources below trace the steps described abve, from transform model architecture to models adapted for biomedical text and that respond to new tasks through prompts:

* [Attention Is All You Need](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762) — the transformer architecture that replaced recurrence with self-attention, becoming the basis for all modern large language models.
* [BERT](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805) and [BioBERT](https://academic.oup.com/bioinformatics/article/36/4/1234/5566506) — a general pretrained model and its adaptation to biomedical text.
* [Language Models are Few-Shot Learners](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.14165) — demonstrated that large language models can perform a wide range of tasks with only a few examples, without task-specific fine-tuning, showing that transformers have general intelligence.
* [Training Language Models to Follow Instructions with Human Feedback](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155) — instruction following and human feedback.
* [Hugging Face's LLM Course, Chapter 1](https://huggingface.co/learn/llm-course/chapter1/1) — an accesible intro to transform models and LLMs.
* [Andrej Karpathy's Intro to LLMs](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zjkBMFhNj_g) — a one-hour talk covering how LLMs work, from tokenization to training to inference. (video)

## 1.1.2 The Rise of RAG (Retrieval Augmented Generation)

A plausible-sounding answer from an LLM is insufficient for answering a scientific question. Researchers need to know which studies support each LLM claim and whether those studies actually address the question being asked.

**Retrieval augmented generation (RAG)** connects a language model to an external collection of documents. When a scientist asks a question, the system retrieves relevant text and supplies it to the model to help it generate an answer. This lets the model work with information provided at the time of the query, including material outside its training data. [Lewis et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401) first demonstrated this approach using retrieved passages from Wikipedia.

With [PaperQA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.07559), FutureHouse introduced RAG for scientific literature by finding full-text articles, assessing relevant pasages, and using them to answer research questions. [PaperQA2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.13740) extended this work to tasks such as synthesizing findings and identifying contradictions in scientific literature. Its development showed why finding the _right_ paper matters: retrieving a soruces does not ensure that the system reads or interprets it correctly.

Retrieval gives an LLM evidence it can use and readers sources they can inspect. The next step is allowing the system to choose when to search, examine a result, and search again. That repeated decision process is central to an AI agent.

### Selected Resources

These resources move from the general RAG approach to systems designed for scientific literature, including FutureHouse's own work:

* [Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401) — introduced Retreival-Augmented Generation (RAG), allowing models to connect to external databases and incorporate new information without needing to be re-trained.
* [PaperQA: Retrieval-Augmented Generative Agent for Scientific Research](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.07559) — retrieval and question answering across scientific articles. (_FutureHouse_)
* [PaperQA2: Language Agents Achieve Superhuman Synthesis of Scientific Knowledge](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.13740) — showed that a language agent optimized for factuality matches or exceeds human subject matter experts on literature research tasks. (_FutureHouse_)
* [Journey to Superhuman Performance on Scientific Tasks](https://www.futurehouse.org/research/engineering-blog-journey-to-superhuman-performance-on-scientific-tasks) — an accesible account of how FutureHouse improved retrieval, including searching a paper's citations and examining retrieval errors. (_FutureHouse_)

## 1.1.3 From Answers to Actions: LLM Agents and Scientific Tools

Retrieving papers helps an LLM answer questions with supporting evidence. But many research tasks require several decisions: search for a study, inspect a results, look up a molecule, run an analysis, and recosnider what to do based on each outcome. 

An **AI agent** uses a language model to choose among available actions in a repeated loop. The model requests an action, software executes it, and the resulting observatin helps the model decide its next step. [ReAct](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.03629) demonstrated this pattern by interleaving a model's reasoning with actions that gather information from external sources.

For scientific research, those actions can be tailored to the research problem. An agent might search a literature database, retrieve a protein record, call a chemistry calculation, or run code to analyze data.

[ChemCrow](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42256-024-00832-8), developed in part by FutureHouse co-founder Andrew White, connected an LLM to expert-designed chemsistry tools. It showed how a model could select tools and use their results across multistep chemistry tasks. The tools supplied capabilities and results that the language model did not have alone.

This shift made it possible to build agents for distinct parts of scientific work rather than asking one model to answer every question from its training. It also created a new challenge: an agent can choose the wrong tool, misread a result, or take an unhelpful sequence of steps. Tesitng the whole process, not just the final answer, became essential.

### Suggested Resources

These papers show how models began choosing and using external tools, first in general tasks and then in a scientific setting:

* [ReAct: Reasoning and Acting in Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.03629) — interleaved reasoning traces with tool use, establishing a pattern for building autonomous agents from LLMs.
* [Toolformer: Language Models Can Teach Themselves to Use Tools](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04761) — a complementary approach that trained a model to decide when and how to call APIs, illustrating how tool use became a model-training question as well as an agent-design question.
* [ChemCrow: Augmenting large-language models with chemistry tools](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42256-024-00832-8) – showed that LLMs equipped with chemistry-specific tools can autonomously plan and execute multi-step chemical tasks, establishing a template for tool-augmented scientific agents. (_FutureHouse_)

## 1.1.4 Measuring Progress on Scientific Tasks

An agent that produces a convincing answer has not necessarily completed a research task correctly. It may have found the wrong paper, misinterpreted a database record, or made an error while analyzing data. To measure progress, researchers need evaluations based on work scientists actually do.

FutureHouse's [LAB-Bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10362) assembled questions involving literature, scientific figures and tables, databases, protocols, and biological sequences. Its successor, [LABBench2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.09554), made these assessments more realisitc and expanded the kinds of research tasks evaluated. Together, they show the shift from testing and what a model knows toward testing whether it can perform useful pieces of biology research.

Evaluating a multistep agent also means observing what happens during a task. [Aviary](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.21154) introduced environments where language agents can take actions, receive results, and be evaluated or trained on scientific priblems, including literature research, molecular cloning, and protein stability. Aviary provided a way to improve agents directly through task experience, making it possible to evaluate and refine each step in an analysis.

As agents began tackling longer analyses, evaluations had to grow with them. [BixBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.00096) tests agents on biological datasets and questions requiring multiple analytical steps. [BixBench3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.25286) goes further: an agent receives a research objective, methodological guidance, and raw data from a published study, then produces analysis artifacts that can be checked against the study's results. This progression makes it possible to measure more of the work involved in a computational biology project while revealing where agents still struggle. A benchmark result remains a measure of its specified tasks, not proof that an agent can conduct every kind of scientific research.

### Suggested Resources

These papers show how FutureHouse moved from measuring individual biology capabilities to evaluating and training agents on multistep scientific work:

* [LAB-Bench: Measuring Capabilities of Language Models for Biology Research](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10362) — practical biology questions across literature, databases, figures, protocols, and sequences. (_FutureHouse_)
* [LABBench2: An Improved Benchmark for AI Systems Performing Biology Research](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.09554) — tasks in more reastic settings, including expanded literature, data-acceess, protocol, and experimental-planning tasks. (_Edison Scientific_)
* [Aviary: Training Language Agents on Challenging Scientific Tasks](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.21154) — introduced Aviary, a framework for multi-step reasoning and tool use across scientific tasks for language agents, and showed that language agents backed by open-source, non-frontier LLMs can match and exceed both frontier LLM agents and human experts on domain-specific scientific tasks. (_FutureHouse_)
* [FutureHouse's Introduction to Aviary](https://www.futurehouse.org/research/aviary) — less technical explanation of why interactive scientific tasks matter for agent training. (_FutureHouse_)
* [BixBench: A Comprehensive Benchmark for LLM-based Agents in Computational Biology](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.00096) — longer bioinformatics tasks that require agents to analyze datasets and interpret results. (_FutureHouse_)
* [BixBench3: Benchmarking AI Agents on Research-Study-Scale Computational Biology Tasks](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.25286) — study-scale analyses starting with raw data and grading the resulting analysis artifacts against those from published studies. (_Edison Scientific_)

## 1.1.5 Experimental Discovery and the Feedback Loop

Scientific discovery is iterative. Researchers use existing knowledge to form a hypothesis, design a test, interpret the result, and revise their next question. Early agent systems began connecting these steps.

In chemistry, [Coscientist](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06792-0) combined language models with search, code, and laboratory automation tools to plan and execute experiments through robotic instruments. This showed one way an agent could interact with a physial laboratory, rather than only produce a proposed experiment in text.

FutureHouse's [Robin](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10652-y) connected this iterative approach to experimental biology. It coordinated literature-search and data-analysis agents to propose therapeutic hypotheses and experiments for dry age-related macular degeneration. Human scientists selected candidates for testing, prepared the protocols, and performed the laboratory experiments; Robin analyzed the resulting data and used those findings to propose follow-up work. The study identified ripasudil as a candidate that increased retinal pigment epithelium phagocytosis in the tested cell models. Robin thus illustrates a **lab-in-the-loop** system: new experimental results that inform the agent's hypotheses.

A related direction is to sustain a longer investigation across many computational steps. [Kosmos](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.02824), developed by Edison Scientific, cycles through literature search, hypothesis generation, and data analysis while keeping a structured record of the investigation.

Together, these systems bring the story from a model answering a question toward agents participating in an iterative research process.

### Suggested Resources

These studies show two way to extend an agent's work beyond a single answer: interacting with laboratory experiments and sustaining a longer computational investigation:

* [Coscientist: Autonomous Chemical Research with Large Language Models](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06792-0) — Coscientist and robotic chemistry experiments.
* [Robin: A Multi-Agent System for Automating Scientific Discovery](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10652-y) — a multi-agent system that automates the full cycle of scientific discovery, from hypothesis generation through experimental design to analysis, demonstrated with real biological experiments. (_FutureHouse_)
* [Kosmos: An AI Scientist for Autonomous Discovery](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.02824) — showed that an AI scientist can autonomously conduct data-driven discovery through iterative cycles of literature search, hypothesis generation, and data analysis, maintaining coherence over extended research trajectories. (_FutureHouse_)
* [FutureHouse's account of Robin](https://www.futurehouse.org/research/demonstrating-end-to-end-scientific-discovery-with-robin-a-multi-agent-system) — a shorter walkthrough of the successive hypotheses, experiments, and analyses. (_FutureHouse_)

## Further Reading

The developments in this chapter did not produce a single, all-purpose "AI scientist." They made different parts of scientific work possible to support and test: understanding research questions, findings evidence, using specialized tools, measuring performance on research takss, and learning from experimental results. These additional resources explore related advanced and offer a broader history for readers who want to go further:

### Broader AI History

* [Ilya Sutskever's Reading List](https://aiconnections.substack.com/p/ilya-sutskevers-reading-list) — a curated set of papers recommended by the OpenAI co-founder for understanding deep learning foundations.
* [An Interview with Alex Krizhevsky](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gwzwkv2hO5k) — Krizhevsky discusses AlexNet and the deep learning revolution in computer vision. (video)
* [Geoffrey Hinton: The 60 Minutes Interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Rl9nHNeketE) — The "godfather of deep learning" on the promise and risks of AI. (video)

### Language Models and Reasoning

* [Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.08361) — showed that model performance improves predictably with more parameters, data, and compute, giving a map for how to improve intelligence of models.
* [Chain-of-Thought Prompting](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.11903) — showed that prompting models to produce intermediate reasoning steps substantially improves performance on complex tasks and that expending more inference compute and give better answers.
* [DeepSeek-R1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12948) — demonstrated the reinforcement learning can break out of the scaling laws and LLMs can improve simply be repeatedly attempting tasks rather than requiring worked-out examples.
* [LLM Pre-Training and Custom LLMs](https://www.databricks.com/blog/llm-pre-training-and-custom-llms) — how LLMs are pre-trained from scratch and adapted for specific domains. (_Databricks_)
* [Aligning Language Models to Follow Instructions](https://openai.com/index/instruction-following/) — OpenAI's blog post on instruction-tuning with RLHF (InstructGPT). (_OpenAI_)
* [A Comprehensive Introduction to Fine-Tuning LLMs](https://medium.com/@sahin.samia/a-comprehensive-introduction-to-fine-tuning-llms-4d1bcc95a83a) — Overview of fine-tuning strategies including LoRA and full fine-tuning. (_Medium_)
* [What is fine-tuning?](https://www.ibm.com/think/topics/fine-tuning) — IBM's explanation of fine-tuning techniques and when to use them. (_IBM_)

### Visual and Hands-On Introductions

### Scientific Models and Agents

* [Deep Learning for Molecules & Materials](https://dmol.pub/index.html#) — Andrew White's open textbook on ML for chemistry and materials. (_FutureHouse_)
* [A Review of Large Language Models and Autonomous Agents in Chemistry](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlelanding/2025/sc/d4sc03921a) — a comprehensive review of how LLMs and autonomous agents are being applied across chemistry. (_FutureHouse_)
* [ether0: Training a Scientific Reasoning Model for Chemistry](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.17238) — demonstrated that reasoning capabilities can be post-trained into LLMs for chemistry without domain-specific pretraining, achieving strong performance with substantially less data than contemporary domain models. (_FutureHouse_)
* [Biomni: Autonomous Biomedical Research with an Artificial Intelligence Agent](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adz4351) — built a general-purpose biomedical agent capable of performing diverse tasks across clinical, genomic, and biomedical domains within a unified framework. (_Stanford_; [arxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.05.30.656746v1))
* [Co-Scientist: Autonomous Chemical Research with Large Language Models](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10644-y) — introduced a multi-agent system built on Gemini that generates and iteratively refines scientific hypotheses using tournament evolution and scaled test-time compute, with experimental validation in drug repurposing for acute myeloid leukemia. (_Google_)
* [The Virtual Lab: AI Agents Design New SARS-CoV-2 Nanobodies with Experimental Validation](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.11.11.623004v1) — an AI-human research collaboration consisting of an LLM principal investigator, a team of LLM agents with different scientific backgrounds, and a human researcher; applied to desgin nanobody binds to recent variants of SARS-CoV-2.
